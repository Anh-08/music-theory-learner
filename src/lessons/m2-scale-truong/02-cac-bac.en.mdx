Number the notes of a scale from 1 to 7 and you get the **scale degrees**. Talking in degrees instead of note names lets you describe a note's *role* regardless of key: degree 5 of C major is G, of G major it is D, but it plays the same part everywhere.

<Show root="C" scale="major" labels="interval" play="run" caption="C major labelled by degree" />

## Names and roles of each degree

| Degree | Name | Feeling |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tonic | Home — complete rest |
| 2 | Supertonic | Floating, wants to go to 1 or up to 3 |
| 3 | Mediant | Stable, defines the major colour |
| 4 | Subdominant | Slightly tense, wants to fall to 3 |
| 5 | Dominant | Stable but "open", like a comma |
| 6 | Submediant | Soft, a little sad, wants to go to 5 |
| 7 | Leading tone | Very tense, wants to rise to 1 right away |

## Hear the pull towards the tonic

Play the scale going up and **stop on degree 7**: <Play notes="C4 D4 E4 F4 G4 A4 B4" />

Uncomfortable, right? Your ear wants the next note. Now let it go home: <Play notes="B4 C5" />

Degree 7 is only a **half step** below degree 1, so it is pulled upwards most strongly — hence the name *leading tone*. Similarly, degree 4 is pulled down to degree 3 (also a half step away): <Play notes="F4 E4" />

<Callout type="tip" title="Your first improvisation secret">
Degrees **1, 3 and 5** are "resting" notes — put them at the end of a phrase or on strong beats and the phrase will sound like it lands somewhere. Degrees **2, 4, 6 and 7** are "passing" notes — use them to move between the resting notes. You will use this principle forever.
</Callout>

## Why talk in numbers?

Musicians say "this song goes 1 – 5 – 6 – 4" instead of "C – G – Am – F", because numbers work in **every key**. Want to transpose? Just know the scale of the new key and swap the numbers for notes.
