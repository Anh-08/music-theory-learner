Triads are "black and white"; seventh chords are "colour". Just stack **one more third** on a triad and you get a fourth note — the **7th** above the root.

## Five kinds of seventh chord

| Name | Symbol | Formula | Root C | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Major 7th | Cmaj7 | 1 – 3 – 5 – 7 | C E G B | Dreamy, smooth (ballads, bossa, jazz) |
| Dominant 7th | C7 | 1 – 3 – 5 – ♭7 | C E G B♭ | Tense, wants to move on (blues, rock) |
| Minor 7th | Cm7 | 1 – ♭3 – 5 – ♭7 | C E♭ G B♭ | Soft, gently sad (soul, R&B) |
| Half-diminished | Cm7♭5 (Cø) | 1 – ♭3 – ♭5 – ♭7 | C E♭ G♭ B♭ | Dark, unsettled |
| Diminished 7th | Cdim7 (C°7) | 1 – ♭3 – ♭5 – ♭♭7 | C E♭ G♭ B♭♭ | Very tense, dramatic |

<Callout type="note" title="Don't confuse maj7 and 7">
"C7" does **not** have a major 7th — it is a **dominant** chord with a *minor* 7th (B♭). For a major 7th (B) you must write **maj7** (or M7, Δ7). This is the most common chord-reading mistake.
</Callout>

## The tritone inside a dominant 7th

In G7 (G B D F), **B** (the 3rd) and **F** (the ♭7) are exactly a **tritone** apart — the most tense interval (Module 1). That is why a dominant 7th always "demands" resolution: B wants to rise to C, F wants to fall to E. <PlayChords chords="G7 C" seconds={1.8} />
