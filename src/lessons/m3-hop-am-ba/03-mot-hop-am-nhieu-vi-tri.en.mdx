The same C chord can be played in many places, with many ways of arranging its notes (**voicings**). Knowing several positions lets you pick the sound that suits the song and change chords with less movement.

<OnlyFor instrument="guitar">

## The CAGED system

The five open chords **C, A, G, E and D** have five different shapes. Here is the magic: replace the nut with your index finger and you can **slide each shape up the neck** to play any chord. And the five shapes **link together** along the neck in exactly the order C – A – G – E – D.

Here are five ways to play a **C** chord, moving up the neck (watch where the red root notes are):

After the D shape comes the C shape an octave higher (fret 12), and the cycle repeats. Put all five together and you get every C – E – G on the neck:

<Callout type="tip" title="How to use CAGED">
The two most used shapes are the **E shape** (root on string 6) and the **A shape** (root on string 5) — the familiar barre chords. Knowing the notes on strings 6 and 5 (Module 0) means you can play every major chord. The other three shapes (C, G, D) are useful higher up the neck, and as a framework for finding scales around a chord when improvising (Module 5).
</Callout>

## Spreading a chord across two hands

A basic accompaniment that always works:

- **Left hand**: the root (optionally adding the octave or the 5th).
- **Right hand**: the triad, choosing the **closest inversion** to the previous chord.

<Show notes="C3 G3 E4 G4 C5" labels="interval" play="chord" caption="An open C: left hand C3 – G3, right hand E4 – G4 – C5 (1st inversion)" />

Spreading the notes like this (**open voicing**) sounds fuller than three notes bunched together (**close voicing**), especially when playing solo.

## Accompanying C – Am – F – G

Left hand plays the root, right hand keeps the inversions as close together as possible:

| Chord | Left hand | Right hand |
|---|---|---|
| C | C3 | C4 – E4 – G4 |
| Am | A2 | C4 – E4 – A4 |
| F | F2 | C4 – F4 – A4 |
| G | G2 | B3 – D4 – G4 |
