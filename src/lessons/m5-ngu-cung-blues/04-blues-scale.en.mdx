## The blue note

Add the **♭5** to the minor pentatonic and you get the **blues scale**:

| | 1 | ♭3 | 4 | ♭5 | 5 | ♭7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A blues | A | C | D | E♭ | E | G |

<Show root="A" scale="blues" labels="interval" />

The ♭5 is very tense (it forms a tritone with the root — Module 1!). Don't linger on it: use it as a **passing note** between 4 and 5, or 5 and 4: <Play notes="D4 Eb4 E4">D – E♭ – E</Play> <Play notes="E4 Eb4 D4 C4 A3">E – E♭ – D – C – A</Play>

## Telling a story with your solo

Knowing the notes is not enough — a good solo **says something**. A few techniques you can use right away:

1. **Motif and repetition**: pick a short phrase of 3 – 5 notes. Play it, then play it again with a small change (change the last note, the rhythm, or move it higher). Listeners love recognising something familiar.
2. **Call and response**: phrase 1 ends unresolved (on ♭7 or 4), phrase 2 "answers" and ends on the root.
3. **Follow the 12-bar form**: each 4-bar line is a "sentence" — line 1 states an idea, line 2 repeats it (over IV7), line 3 concludes.
4. **Less is more**: one long note in the right place beats ten rushed ones.

<OnlyFor instrument="guitar">

5. **Bends and slides**: bending the ♭3 up slightly (not quite to the 3) is the classic blues sound. Bend the 4 up a whole step to reach the 5. Slide into notes instead of fretting them directly.

</OnlyFor>

<OnlyFor instrument="piano">

5. **Grace notes**: quickly strike the ♭3 and slide up to the 3 (C → C♯ over A7) — the piano can't bend, but this creates a similar effect. Tremolo between two notes for a driving phrase.

</OnlyFor>

## The blues jam

A 12-bar blues in A. Start with the minor pentatonic, then add the ♭5. Watch the instrument: when the chord changes to D7, D becomes the red note — ending a phrase there sounds very "in the form".

<Jam progression="A7*4 D7*2 A7*2 E7 D7 A7 E7" songKey="A" groove="blues" tempo={88} scale="blues" />

<Callout type="tip" title="Next step">
Over the A7 chord, the C (♭3 of the minor pentatonic) rubs gently against C♯ (the 3rd of the chord) — that rub is the blues colour. Want something sweeter? Choose **A major pentatonic** in the scale list (it has C♯) and compare. Mixing the two pentatonics is the secret of many blues players — and will make much more sense after Modules 6 – 7 on 7th chords and chord tones.
</Callout>
