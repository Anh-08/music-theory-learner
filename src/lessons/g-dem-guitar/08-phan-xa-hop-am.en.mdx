When you practise a song you know, your hands **memorise** its path. With a new chord sheet you have to read and change at the same time. This lesson trains exactly that: chords appear one after another to a metronome and **never repeat a fixed loop**, so there is nothing to memorise.

The chords are still chosen so they **connect naturally** (V back to I, vi to IV, ii to V…) and every phrase resolves to the tonic. That keeps long sessions pleasant to hear, and your ear gradually learns how chords go together.

## How to practise

1. **Look one chord ahead.** The chord on the right is the next one. While you play the current chord, your eyes are already reading the next.
2. **Move on the last beat.** Start lifting your hand on the last beat of the old chord so that beat 1 of the new chord lands on time. It is fine if that last beat sounds a little thin.
3. **Keep common fingers down.** Many chord pairs share fingers that stay put — C → Am keeps fingers 1 and 2. Don't lift them.
4. **Don't stop.** If you fumble a chord, let it go and catch the next one. Keeping time matters more than every note ringing.

<Callout type="tip" title="Two ways to play">
**Strum on each beat**: one downstroke per metronome click — the easiest way to practise the changes. **One bar of a rhythm**: play exactly one bar of the rhythm you choose (Ballad, Slow, Waltz…) on each chord, just like accompanying a song.
</Callout>

## Try it

Start on **Easy** (4 beats per chord, 70 bpm). Once you change in time without stumbling, move to **Medium**: 2 beats per chord, with some seventh chords.

Once the common loops feel comfortable, switch the source to **Generated**: new progressions are created continuously from chord functions, 4 or 8 chords per phrase. **Hard** hides the shapes and now and then moves to a nearby key — just like meeting an unfamiliar song.
