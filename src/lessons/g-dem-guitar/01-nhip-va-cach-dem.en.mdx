Before learning rhythms, there is something more important than any strumming pattern: **feeling and counting the beat**. A good accompanist is not someone who strums fast, but someone who **never drifts off the beat**.

## Beats and bars

- The **beat** is the steady pulse of a song — where you naturally tap your foot.
- Beats are grouped into **bars** (measures). The first beat of a bar is the **downbeat**, and it usually coincides with a chord change.

The **time signature** tells you how many beats are in a bar:

| Time | Count | Feel | Common in |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4/4 | **1** 2 3 4 | Steady, the most common | Pop, ballads, rock, disco, bolero |
| 3/4 | **1** 2 3 | Swaying "strong – weak – weak" | Waltz, Boston |
| 2/4 | **1** 2 | Driving, like footsteps | Marches, some tangos |
| 12/8 | **1** 2 3 **2** 2 3 **3** 2 3 **4** 2 3 | 4 beats, each split in three | Slow rock, slow blues |

## Subdividing the beat

A strum can land on a beat or **between beats**. We count subdivisions out loud:

- **Eighth notes** — the beat split in two: "**1** & **2** & **3** & **4** &".
- **Sixteenth notes** — split in four: "**1** e & a **2** e & a…".
- **Triplets** — split in three: "**1** · · **2** · · …" (say "1 – trip – let").

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep your strumming hand moving">
Even when a rhythm has rests, **your strumming hand keeps moving up and down** like a pendulum: down on the beat, up on the "&". A rest is just your hand passing without touching the strings (a ghost strum). This is the secret to never losing the beat.
</Callout>

## Strong beats, weak beats and syncopation

In 4/4, beat **1** is the strongest, beat **3** is medium, beats **2** and **4** are weak. Pop and rock, however, often **accent beats 2 and 4** (the snare drum) — which creates a "bouncy" feel.

**Syncopation** means accenting an unexpected place — between the beats. For example the Ballad pattern "**D** – D U – U D U": the upstroke on the "& of 2" carries over a silent beat 3, creating a lovely floating feel.
