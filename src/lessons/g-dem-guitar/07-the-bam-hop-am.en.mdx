A chord sheet says "C" but not where to play it. Knowing **several shapes** for the same chord lets you: change chords with shorter moves, choose a tone colour (open shapes ring, high shapes sound bright and tight), and play "unusual" chords like Cmaj9, G13 or E7♯9.

## Reading a chord diagram

- **Vertical lines** are the strings: **string 6** (low E) on the left, **string 1** on the right.
- **Horizontal lines** are frets. The thick bar at the top is the nut; if the diagram says **"5fr"**, the first box is fret 5.
- **Numbered dots** show where to press; the number is the suggested **finger**: 1 = index, 2 = middle, 3 = ring, 4 = little finger. Red dots are the **root**.
- **A horizontal bar** is a **barre** (one finger pressing several strings).
- **×** is a muted string, **o** an open string.

You will recognise the five **CAGED** shapes (Module 3): C, A, G, E and D shapes linking up the neck. Tips for accompanying songs:

- Choose the shape of the next chord that sits closest to the one you are holding → less hand movement, chord changes in time.
- Playing with another guitarist? One plays open shapes, the other high shapes — the guitars stay distinct instead of "crowding" each other.

## 9th, 11th and 13th chords

A guitar only has 6 strings — and you only have so many fingers — so chords with many notes must **leave some out**. The usual order of omission:

1. **The perfect 5th** — it barely changes the chord's colour.
2. **The 9th** in 11th / 13th chords.
3. **The 11th** in 13th chords.

Always keep: **the root, the 3rd, the 7th and the highest extension** — those notes give the chord its "name". The Chord shapes page lists which notes may be left out for every chord.
