In Module 5 you improvised with one scale for the whole song. It sounded fine, but sometimes the lines "drift" without connecting to the chords. The secret of good soloists: **each chord has its own notes — chord tones** (root, 3rd, 5th, 7th) — and they aim for them.

## The scale is vocabulary, chord tones are anchors

- **The scale** tells you which notes are "not wrong".
- **Chord tones** tell you which notes are "most right" **right now** — because they match the chord that is sounding.

The golden rule: **put chord tones on strong beats** (beats 1 and 3), especially **beat 1 when the chord changes**. Use the other scale notes to move between them, on weak beats.

## Exercise 2: connect chord tones with scale notes

Now play a chord tone on beat 1, then walk with scale notes (the grey notes) to **the nearest chord tone of the next chord**. For example from C to Am: E (3rd of C) → D → C (♭3 of Am). From Am to F: C → C (just hold it — C is the 5th of F!).

<Callout type="tip" title="Common tones are bridges">
Neighbouring chords often share **common tones**: C and Am share C and E; Am and F share A and C; F and G share nothing — you have to move. Holding a common tone while the chord changes sounds very "intentional".
</Callout>

## Notes to avoid

Some scale notes **clash when placed on a strong beat** of a particular chord: usually the note a **half step above** a chord tone. For example over Cmaj7, **F** (a half step above E) sounds very tense if held. Used as a passing note it is fine.
