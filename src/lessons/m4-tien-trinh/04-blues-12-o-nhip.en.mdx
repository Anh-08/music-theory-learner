If you learn only one song form for jamming, make it the **12-bar blues**. It uses just three chords, repeats forever, and every musician in the world knows it. From *Johnny B. Goode* to countless rock songs, this form is everywhere.

## The structure

Each box is one bar of 4 beats. Three lines of 4 bars each:

| | Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Line 1 | I7 | I7 | I7 | I7 |
| Line 2 | IV7 | IV7 | I7 | I7 |
| Line 3 | V7 | IV7 | I7 | V7 |

The last bar (V7) is called the **turnaround** — it "turns" you back to the top of the form.

In A: I7 = **A7**, IV7 = **D7**, V7 = **E7**.

<Callout type="note" title="The blues bends the rules a little">
According to Module 3, the I and IV chords in a major key are major triads, not 7th chords. The blues deliberately uses **7th chords on all three degrees** — that slight clash is what creates the blues colour. It is also why we improvise over the blues with the **minor pentatonic** even though the chords are major (Module 5).
</Callout>

## The shuffle feel

Blues is often played with a **shuffle** feel: each beat is divided into three, and the first note is twice as long as the second — "**long**-short **long**-short". Listen to the hi-hat and bass in the backing track above. The bass plays the classic "boogie" pattern: 1 – 3 – 5 – 6 – ♭7 – 6 – 5 – 3.

## The "quick change" variation

A very common variation: move to IV7 already in **bar 2**, then back to I7:
