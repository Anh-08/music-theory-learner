V7 pulls strongly to I. The idea of secondary dominants: **give each diatonic chord its own "V7"** — a dominant 7th chord placed right before it, a fifth above it.

## Secondary dominants in C major

| Secondary dominant | Symbol | Leads to |
|---|---|---|
| A7 | V7/ii | Dm |
| B7 | V7/iii | Em |
| C7 | V7/IV | F |
| D7 | V7/V | G |
| E7 | V7/vi | Am |

<Callout type="tip" title="How to spot a secondary dominant">
See a **major or 7th chord that doesn't belong to the key**, followed by a chord **a fifth lower** (moving around the circle of fifths)? It is almost certainly a secondary dominant. For example in C: E7 → Am, A7 → Dm.
</Callout>

## Improvising over secondary dominants

A secondary dominant brings a **foreign note** — the leading tone of its target chord: E7 has G♯ (leading tone of A), A7 has C♯ (leading tone of D). Over that chord:

- Aim for **the 3rd of the secondary dominant** (G♯ over E7), then resolve it up a half step (to A).
- Or use the Mixolydian / the target's harmonic minor (A harmonic minor over E7).
