A **borrowed chord** comes from the **parallel key** (same tonic, major ↔ minor). Most common of all: a major-key song borrowing chords from its parallel minor.

## Common borrowed chords in C major (from C minor)

| Chord | Symbol | Effect | Listen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fm | iv | Bittersweet, nostalgic | <PlayChords chords="C F Fm C" /> |
| A♭ | ♭VI | Warm, wide, a little "cinematic" | <PlayChords chords="C Ab G C" /> |
| B♭ | ♭VII | Rock, strong | <PlayChords chords="C Bb F C" /> |
| E♭ | ♭III | Surprising, light and dark mixed | <PlayChords chords="C Eb F C" /> |

The progression **♭VI – ♭VII – I** (A♭ – B♭ – C) sounds very epic — often used at a climax or at the end of a song: <PlayChords chords="C Am Ab Bb C" />

<Callout type="note" title="The minor iv: a magic 'sad note'">
F → Fm changes just one note: A falls to A♭. That A♭ (the ♭6 of C) creates a very particular "sweet but painful" feeling — hundreds of ballads use this trick at the end of a phrase.
</Callout>

## Improvising over borrowed chords

A borrowed chord brings notes from the minor key: Fm has A♭, B♭ has B♭, A♭ has A♭ and E♭. When you reach that chord, **swap the matching note** in your scale (use A♭ instead of A over Fm), or simply target its chord tones.
