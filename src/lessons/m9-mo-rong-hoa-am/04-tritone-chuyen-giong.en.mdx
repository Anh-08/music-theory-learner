## Tritone substitution

G7 (G B D F) and **D♭7** (D♭ F A♭ C♭) share two notes: **B (= C♭) and F** — exactly the tritone pair that gives a dominant chord its pull. So D♭7 can **replace** G7 on the way to C. Their roots are a tritone apart — hence **tritone substitution**.

The effect: the bass slides down by half steps, very smoothly: **Dm7 – D♭7 – Cmaj7** (D – D♭ – C).

## Passing diminished chords

A **dim7** chord slipped between two chords a whole step apart creates a bass line moving by half steps: **C – C♯dim7 – Dm7**, or **Dm7 – D♯dim7 – Em7**. Listen: <PlayChords chords="C C#dim7 Dm7 G7" />

<Callout type="note" title="dim7 is symmetrical">
A dim7 chord is built from equally stacked minor thirds, so **moving it 3 frets gives the same chord** (C♯dim7 = Edim7 = Gdim7 = B♭dim7). There are only 3 different dim7 chords!
</Callout>

## Modulation

**Pivot-chord modulation:** find a chord that belongs to both keys. From C to G: Am is vi in C and also ii in G. Go C → Am (now heard as ii of G) → D7 → G, and you have arrived in G.

**Direct modulation:** raise the whole song by a half or whole step for the final chorus — a familiar way to build a climax in pop: <PlayChords chords="C G Am F D A Bm G" seconds={1} />
