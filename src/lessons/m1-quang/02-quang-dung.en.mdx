## "Perfect" intervals

4ths, 5ths and octaves are not major or minor — their quality is **perfect**, because they sound so consonant, open and solid.

| Interval | Half steps | From C | Listen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect 4th (4) | 5 | C – F | <Play notes="C4 F4" as="both" /> |
| Perfect 5th (5) | 7 | C – G | <Play notes="C4 G4" as="both" /> |
| Perfect octave (8) | 12 | C – C | <Play notes="C4 C5" as="both" /> |

Raise a perfect interval by a half step and it becomes **augmented**; lower it by a half step and it becomes **diminished**.

## The tritone: the most tense interval

Between the perfect 4th (5 half steps) and the perfect 5th (7 half steps) sits an interval of **6 half steps = 3 whole tones** — hence the name **tritone**. Depending on spelling it is an **augmented 4th** (C – F♯) or a **diminished 5th** (C – G♭).

Listen: <Play notes="C4 F#4" as="both">C – F♯</Play>. Very tense, as if it wants to "run" somewhere. The tritone lives inside every dominant 7th chord (like G7) and is what pulls G7 towards C — you will meet it again in Module 6.

<Callout type="note" title="B – F: the only tritone on the white keys">
Among the natural notes, every 5th is a perfect 5th — **except B – F** (only 6 half steps, a diminished 5th). This pair produces the only diminished chord in the key of C major.
</Callout>

## Power chords: 1 + 5

<OnlyFor instrument="guitar">

A rock power chord is simply the **root + a perfect 5th** (plus the octave for weight). There is no 3rd, so it is neither major nor minor — which is why it works almost anywhere.

<Show root="G" notes="G D" shape="355xxx" labels="interval" caption="G5: string 6 fret 3 (G) – string 5 fret 5 (D) – string 4 fret 5 (G)" />

Perfect 5th shape: **next string up, 2 frets higher**. Perfect 4th shape: **next string, same fret** (again except the G–B pair: one fret higher).

</OnlyFor>

<OnlyFor instrument="piano">

A left hand playing **root + 5th** is the most basic bass accompaniment: solid and clear without getting muddy. Try it with C and G:

<Show notes="C3 G3" labels="interval" play="chord" caption="A perfect 5th in the left hand: C3 – G3" />

On the white keys, a perfect 5th always has **3 white keys in between** — except B – F.

</OnlyFor>

## 4ths and 5ths are two sides of the same coin

C up to G is a 5th. G up to C (the C above) is a 4th. Together they make an octave. This is called **interval inversion** — the next lesson covers it in more detail.
