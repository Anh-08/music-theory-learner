The melody is the part listeners sing along to. The good news: writing a melody uses exactly the skills you practised while improvising (Modules 5 – 7) — just slower and more selective.

## Five principles

1. **Contour**: good phrases often have an **arch** shape — rising to a high point, then falling. Save the highest note of the song for the chorus.
2. **Move mostly by step**, with occasional **leaps** — and after a big leap, **step back the other way**: <Play notes="C4 D4 E4 A4 G4 F4 E4">C D E ↗A G F E</Play>
3. **Chord tones on strong beats** (Module 7): the note on beat 1 belongs to the current chord; other notes go on weak beats.
4. **Motif and repetition**: a short idea repeated and varied with the chords — listeners will remember it.
5. **Hook**: a short, singable phrase placed in the chorus and repeated several times.

## An example over C – G – Am – F

Phrase 2 (answer), ending on C — resolved: <Play notes="C4 C4 E4 C4 A3 C4 D4 C4">phrase 2</Play>

Notice the first note of each bar: E (in C), D (in G), C (in Am), A (in F) — all chord tones.

## Lyrics in tonal languages

If you write lyrics in a **tonal language** such as Vietnamese (6 tones) or Mandarin, the melody has to "follow" the words, or they will be heard as different words:

- **Rising tones** suit rising or high notes.
- **Falling / low tones** suit falling or low notes.
- **Level tones** are the most flexible.

The method: read the lyric out loud many times, notice its natural "speech melody", then exaggerate it into a musical melody. (In English, match the **stressed syllables** to strong beats instead.)
