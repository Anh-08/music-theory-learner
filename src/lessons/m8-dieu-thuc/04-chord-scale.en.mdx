## Every diatonic chord is a mode

In C major, each diatonic seventh chord corresponds to the mode starting on that degree:

| Chord | Mode | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 (I) | C Ionian | C D E F G A B |
| Dm7 (ii) | D Dorian | D E F G A B C |
| Em7 (iii) | E Phrygian | E F G A B C D |
| Fmaj7 (IV) | F Lydian | F G A B C D E |
| G7 (V) | G Mixolydian | G A B C D E F |
| Am7 (vi) | A Aeolian | A B C D E F G |
| Bm7♭5 (vii) | B Locrian | B C D E F G A |

Look at the "Notes" column: **they are all the seven notes of C major!** So over a diatonic progression you can play the key's scale — the modes are just a way of **landing on each chord's chord tones** in the right places.

## A quick chart by chord type

When you meet a chord **outside the key**, or a long vamp, pick a mode by chord type:

| Chord type | Common mode |
|---|---|
| maj7 | Ionian, or Lydian (a modern colour) |
| 7 (dominant) | Mixolydian |
| m7 | Dorian (most common), Aeolian or Phrygian depending on the key |
| m7♭5 | Locrian |

## Two ways of thinking when improvising

1. **Think in keys**: play the key's scale and target each chord's chord tones (Module 7). Simple, and enough for most pop songs.
2. **Think in modes**: for each chord, think of a mode rooted on that chord. Useful when chords keep changing key (jazz) or over a one-chord vamp.

Try it on ii – V – I: think "D Dorian → G Mixolydian → C Ionian". They are all white keys, but your phrases will naturally revolve around each chord's root.
